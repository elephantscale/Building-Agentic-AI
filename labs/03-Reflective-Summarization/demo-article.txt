INTERNAL FIELD REPORT — "Project Harbor" agentic rollout, Meridian Logistics (draft, lightly edited)

Some background first, because people keep asking. Meridian Logistics started in 1998 as a
regional trucking broker in Ohio, went through two private-equity owners, and only spun up a
real software group after the 2019 warehouse-management rewrite. The dispatch team still jokes
about the old green-screen terminals, which is relevant mostly because it explains why half the
operations data lived in CSV exports nobody trusted. Anyway. The Project Harbor pilot ran for
eleven weeks across the Columbus and Memphis hubs, and this note is the rough internal writeup
before the steering review.

The pilot put an LLM-based assistant in front of the exception-handling desk — the people who
deal with late shipments, mis-scans, and customs holds. Before Harbor, an exception took an
agent a median of 19 minutes to resolve end to end. With the assistant drafting the resolution
and pulling the relevant tracking and contract data, the median dropped to 7 minutes, a 63%
reduction. The mean fell less dramatically, from 26 to 14 minutes, because a long tail of nasty
customs cases didn't improve at all — the assistant was basically useless on anything involving
international paperwork, and a couple of agents said it made those cases slower because they
felt obliged to read its wrong suggestion first.

Volume context: the two hubs handle roughly 3,100 exceptions a week between them, about 1,900 in
Memphis and 1,200 in Columbus. During the pilot the assistant was offered on 71% of eligible
exceptions (the rest were routed to specialty desks it wasn't cleared for). Agents accepted its
draft resolution as-is 38% of the time, edited it before sending 44% of the time, and threw it
away entirely 18% of the time. That 18% discard rate is the number the steering committee will
fixate on, and honestly they should.

Cost. This is where it gets muddy and I want to be careful. The model API itself came to about
$0.14 per exception handled. But the real number people should quote is the fully-loaded figure
— including the vector search infrastructure, the data-sync jobs, and the on-call engineer we
had to keep during the pilot — which works out closer to $0.41 per exception. Someone in finance
has been quoting the $0.14 and it's going to cause a fight. For scale: at 3,100 exceptions a week
the fully-loaded cost is roughly $66,000 a year per hub, before any enterprise discount.

A tangent that came up: the same platform team also demoed a warehouse slotting optimizer last
quarter that saved an estimated 4% on pick travel time. It has nothing to do with Harbor, but
executives keep bundling them in the same breath, so expect that to surface in the review and try
to keep it separate.

The failure modes worth naming. First, the assistant confidently fabricated a carrier contract
clause in three documented cases — it invented a penalty cap that wasn't in the contract. Nobody
sent those out, but only because an agent happened to catch it. Second, automation bias: in the
edited-44% bucket, spot checks suggest agents were rubber-stamping the assistant's framing even
when they changed the wording, so the "edit" was cosmetic. Third, the assistant's performance
degraded noticeably in the last two weeks, which we traced to a stale data-sync job, not the
model — a good reminder that these systems fail from the plumbing, not just the prompt.

On governance: every resolution still required a human to click send; nothing auto-dispatched.
We logged all 14,200-odd assistant interactions to an audit table, though the schema was
inconsistent for the first week and that data is partly unusable. Legal wants a hard rule that any
assistant output touching a contract clause gets a second human reviewer before it goes out, and
I agree.

Recommendation, tentative: expand to the Dallas hub but gate it — keep the assistant off
international/customs exceptions entirely, fix the data-sync monitoring before anything else, quote
the $0.41 fully-loaded number not the $0.14, and put the contract-clause two-reviewer rule in
before expansion, not after. The 63% median improvement is real and worth having. The 18% discard
rate and the fabricated-clause incidents are why this is a gated expansion and not a victory lap.
