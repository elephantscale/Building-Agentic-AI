Agentic AI in Customer Support: A Field Report
Northwind Software — Internal Engineering Note, September 2026

Over the past two quarters our support organization piloted an agentic assistant to help
tier-1 agents triage and answer inbound tickets. This note summarizes what we built, what the
numbers showed, and where the approach fell short. It is written for engineers considering a
similar rollout, not as a marketing document.

Background. Northwind handles roughly 4,200 support tickets per week across email and chat.
Before the pilot, median first-response time was 5 hours and 12 minutes, and the reopen rate —
tickets a customer had to escalate a second time — sat at 18 percent. Tier-1 agents spent an
estimated 40 percent of their time on repetitive triage: reading the ticket, categorizing it,
and finding the relevant help-center article. That triage work was the target.

What we built. The assistant is an LLM in a loop with three tools: a read-only help-center
search, a read-only CRM lookup keyed by account ID, and a draft-reply generator. Critically,
the assistant never sends anything. It produces a draft, a category, a priority, and a set of
cited sources, and a human agent approves or edits before the reply goes out. Every tool call
and every draft is written to an append-only audit log. We treated all retrieved text as
untrusted data rather than as instructions, after an early red-team test showed a ticket body
could otherwise smuggle in commands.

Results. Over an eight-week measured period, median first-response time fell from 5 hours 12
minutes to 2 hours 48 minutes, a 46 percent reduction. The reopen rate improved modestly, from
18 percent to 15 percent. Agent satisfaction with the tool was high: in an internal survey, 71
percent of tier-1 agents said the drafts saved them time on a typical ticket. Cost was
non-trivial — the assistant added roughly 0.11 US dollars per ticket in model spend, which at
our volume is about 460 dollars per week.

Where it fell short. Three failure modes recurred. First, on ambiguous tickets that bundled
several requests, the assistant tended to answer the first ask and ignore the rest; we now flag
multi-ask tickets for mandatory human review. Second, the draft quality on billing disputes was
weaker than on technical questions, because our help-center billing content was thin. The model
is only as grounded as the source it can cite. Third, and most important, agents occasionally
approved a draft without reading it closely, trusting the tool too much. Automation bias is
real, and a human gate only works if the human actually reviews.

Recommendations. Start read-only. Keep a human approval step on anything that sends, refunds,
or changes a record. Invest in the underlying knowledge base before investing in the model —
grounding beats cleverness. Measure a frozen set of tickets before and after, not anecdotes.
And design the human gate so that skipping the review is harder than doing it. The technology
worked; the discipline around it is what made it safe.
